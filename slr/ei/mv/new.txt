Abstract
This dissertation investigates the “intent–action gap” in student innovation-oriented venture creation within the macro-institutional environment of the Hong Kong (“HK”)—Greater Bay Area (“GBA”) innovation corridor. While traditional cognitive frameworks like the theory of planned behaviour (“TPB”) enjoy baseline localised validity, a systematic literature review (“SLR”) reveals an empirical vacuum regarding how external structures moderate the translation of intentions into active entrepreneurial performance. To resolve this structural gap, this study constructs a behavioural core mapping how cultural distortions, high alternative corporate reservation wages, and cognitive biases alter traditional career choices, using affective engines like entrepreneurial passion (“EP”) to override economic utility boundaries. Grounded in post-positivist, socio-cognitive frameworks, the study deploys an explanatory sequential mixed-methods research architecture. Quantitative survey sampling (n≥135) is paired with targeted qualitative access brokered via institutional channels to operationalise context-adapted instruments across a multi-stage execution timeline. By benchmarking the regional milieu against mature Western ecosystem templates, the analysis demonstrates how cross-boundary GBA infrastructure support (“GIS”), technology transfer office (“TTO”) interventions, and multi-stakeholder alignments structurally mitigate localised Kirznerian market friction and lower failure stigma. Ultimately, this applied doctoral inquiry departs from isolated intention studies to offer a three-dimensional contribution to contemporary entrepreneurship theory, regional institutional analysis, and open-science university incubation methodologies.



1.2.	Background
Innovation-oriented entrepreneurship acts as a primary catalyst for economic dynamism, and long-term structural competitiveness, (Agarwal and Shah, 2014). While nascent founders prioritise immediate profitability over system innovation (Hobsbawm, 1969), Schumpeterian (2015; 2017) creation theory (“CRT”) posits that radical “creative destruction” is required to disrupt market equilibria and catalyse deep industrial transformations. Conversely, Kirznerian (1973) discovery theory (“DIS”) suggests that opportunity recognition requires entrepreneurial alertness rather than radical innovativeness, thereby facilitating arbitrage, imitation, and rapid market entry. When widely replicated within an ecosystem; however a systemic reliance on passive discovery risks rapid market saturation and limited strategic differentiation (Kellermanns, 2008; Sirmon et al., 2008).

As a financial and commercial hub with world-class institutional infrastructure, HK provides a compelling context for examining this tension between discovery-driven imitation and innovation-oriented creation (Liu, 2022); yet, its start-up ecosystem performance has historically lagged behind global benchmarks (Yeung, 2015). Grounded in institutional theory (“INS”) (North, 1990), macro-level economic realities heavily shape individual career pathways (Baumol, 1990).

According to the StartupBlink Global Startup Ecosystem Index (“GSEI”) 2026, HK’s macro-ecosystem has broken its multi-year stagnation (see Table 1), ascending to 35th globally and reclaiming 4th place nationally. This upward trajectory is underpinned by a 26.6% annual surge in absolute ecosystem score. While these macroeconomic metrics highlight robust systemic capitalisation—reflected by over 1,380 active high-growth enterprise—they simultaneously expose the core paradox: institutional friction preventing this massive capital infusions from naturally activating a corresponding volume of organic student venture creations. This macro-level expansion is further triangulated by complementary data from Startup Genome (2025), which highlights micro-foundational momentum including a doubling of venture exits exceeding HKD 50 million, an expanding base of 4,694 active startups (a 10% year-on-year increase), and clear regional leadership in financial technology.

Severe structural and environmental constraints underpin this paradox (Nichols and Tang, 2022). Exorbitant living costs, high rental expenses, and risk-averse cultural preferences heavily diminish the perceived feasibility of high-growth venture creation (Siu and Lo, 2013). Furthermore, lucrative corporate wages within HK’s hyper-free economy artificially inflate a graduate’s reservation wage, creating immense opportunity costs that drive young adults toward stable, prestigious salaried employment rather than high-uncertainty startup trajectories (Lau, 2019; Lo et al., 2023).

Consequently, academic research on local student populations (Liang et al., 2019; Siu and Lo, 2013; Sun et al., 2017) has predominantly centred on entrepreneurial intention (“EI”), treating intentions as the singular proximal antecedent of planned behaviour. Interestingly, an empirical study on Stanford University indicates that entrepreneurship education (“EE”) has no significant effects on EI (Eesley and Lee, 2021). Systemically, meta-appraisal in Chapter 2 has uncovered a regional conceptual fragmentation: localised HK studies remain restricted to static applications of the theory of planned behaviour (“TPB”) (Ajzen, 1991), repeatedly verifying that internal attitudes and subjective norms predict intent (Fini et al., 2012).

Crucially, this cognitive hyper-focus ignores the reality that intention formation and its hands-on execution extend far beyond cold, calculated cognitive processes (Barrett et al., 2007; Cardon et al., 2012). Global theoretical benchmarks increasingly emphasise entrepreneurial passion (“EP”)—defined as intense positive feelings toward entrepreneurial activities that are deeply intertwined with a founder’s core personal identity—as the vital affective engine driving real-world motivation, persistence, and goal commitment (Baum and Locke, 2004; Cardon et al., 2013). 

Within HK’s student corpus, this affective domain remains critically unexamined. While a single, isolated regional study has recently begun to acknowledge the role of passion as an institutional antecedent (Hou et al., 2023), it stands as an anomaly in a literature otherwise dominated by static, cognitive TPB frameworks. This baseline hyper-focus fails to explain why HK undergraduates exhibit significantly limited EI compared to their counterparts in the broader Greater Bay Area (“GBA”), a phenomenon heavily tied to the high opportunity costs of local self-employment (Bickenbach et al., 2017; McMullen et al., 2008).

By allowing static, individual-level predictors to overshadow these vital affective and environmental dynamics, the regional literature suffers from a profound “intent–action gap”, failing to explain how passive student intentions are actually translated into active market entries. This dissertation directly addresses this theoretical gap. By integrating multi-dimensional EP constructs with macro-level ecosystem variables into an advanced socio-cognitive framework, this study provides a robust, ecosystem-mediated explanation of innovation-oriented entrepreneurial behaviours among HK institutional cohorts. 

1.3.	Research Title
From Intention to Creation: Entrepreneurial Passion and Ecosystem Support in Hong Kong

1.4.	Problem Statement
Despite HK’s world-class financial infrastructure and its recent macroeconomic acceleration up to 27th globally in international ecosystem rankings (Startup Genome, 2025), a deep structural paradox remains unresolved (Mills, 2012). The region’s startup landscape continues to lag behind global comparators in innovation-led venture density (StartupBlink, 2025). This macro-level disparity stems from a critical micro-foundational bottleneck: a profound “intent–action gap” among the city’s highly educated youth, where passive student career intentions fail to materialise into active, high-growth venture creation (Liu, 2022).

This persistent gap highlights two deep systemic mismatches between existing regional research and the real-world constraints faced by nascent student innovators:
•	Affective and empirical silo: By computing intention as a cold, purely cognitive process and leaving emotional passion critically unexamined, localised literature remains confined within traditional applications of TPB.
•	Institutional lacuna: Contemporary foundational paradigms—specifically INS and knowledge spillover theory (“KST”)—possess near-zero empirical representation within HK’s student entrepreneurship contexts.

This theoretical and empirical deficit leaves university administrators, incubators, and regional policymakers without a clear, evidence-based strategy to translate student ideas into genuine economic creation.

This dissertation introduces a multi-level, ecosystem-mediated framework that moves past generic, homogeneous intention models to address three critical domains:
•	Theoretically: It stands as the first multi-level regional research mapping the trajectory from individual personality filters through the EP engine to final behavioural outcomes.
•	Contextually: It provides data-driven insights into how hyper-free market realities shape individual career choices during HK’s strategic integration with the GBA.
•	Practically: The findings deliver an actionable structural blueprint for universities and policymakers to engineer interventions that effectively bridge the “intent–action gap”.

Theoretical Foundations: The Evolution from Cognitive Intent to Affective Drive
Epistemological Foundations and Cognitive Models
To map the complex path of student career selection, the wider literature must first be categorised by its underlying epistemological frameworks. As systematically reviewed by van Burg and Romme (2014), entrepreneurship research maps onto three classical Aristotelian epistemic domains: 
	Episteme: Scientific, positivist laws of discovery (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000)
	Phronesis: Constructivist, practical wisdom found in narrative lived experiences (Cornelissen and Clarke, 2010)
	Techne: Instrumental design-science reasoning, such as effectuation (Sarasvathy, 2004)
This dissertation bridges these domains by deploying a post-positivist quantitative path core (episteme) complemented by a constructivist qualitative lens (phronesis) to examine the “intent–action gap” in its native context.

Historically, this path was evaluated through purely cognitive frameworks—chiefly the TPB—which treat the decision to start a business as a static, homogeneous calculation of ATE and PBC (Ajzen, 1991). While this baseline cognitive structure enjoys widespread validity, contemporary scholarship proves that its psychometric predictors undergo severe distortions when transposed into non-Western cultural environments. Consistently, Siu and Lo (2013) demonstrate that individualist–collectivist cultural dimensions significantly moderate the relationship between a student’s ESE and their subsequent EI, revealing that in collectivist Chinese settings, individual confidence remains heavily bound by relational and societal expectations. This cross-cultural variance is further reinforced by Liñán and Chen (2009), whose structural equation modelling (n=519) across Spain and Taiwan confirms that the predictive weight of psychometric antecedents aligns strictly with regional cultural dimensions (Hofstede, 1984).

Within HK-specific geographic confines, this cognitive foundation remains highly relevant; Zhang et al. (2019) via an empirical sample of local university students (n=200) that structured entrepreneurial training programs successfully drive EI through the direct activation of ATE, SN, and PBC. However, these traditional models remain incomplete because they evaluate abstract cognitive intentions in isolation, completely failing to explain how these intentions shift into action when confronted with severe economic trade-offs.

Behavioural Economics and Job Selection Boundary
To resolve this “intent–action gap”, contemporary literature has increasingly infused foundational insights from behavioural economics, modelling the venture entry choice not as an unconstrained choice, but as an intense psychological conflict between alternative career pathways. According to traditional labour economics, regional markets characterised by high corporate wages significantly elevate a graduate’s reservation wage—the minimum guaranteed financial compensation required to forgo traditional employment (McMullen et al., 2008). In competitive metropolitan hubs, rational students facing lucrative corporate options will systematically abandon the entrepreneurial first step if they accurately calculate the low statistical odds of venture success.

Monsen et al. (2010) operationalise this tension through utility-maximisation theory (Casson, 2003; Douglas and Shepherd, 2000), demonstrating that a student’s willingness to perform entrepreneurial actions is a direct trade-off between profit-sharing incentives and the psychological disutility of risk exposure. This structural status-quo bias is further compounded by a candidate’s professional background; Sorensen (2007) leverages the Integrated Database for Labour Market Research (“IDA”) to prove that experience within highly structured, bureaucratic corporate environments exerts a profound negative influence on nascent activity, locking individuals into stable employment paths.

Conversely, economic shocks can violently disrupt this rational baseline. While Schumpeterian creative destruction assumes a macro-level organizational churn (Decker et al., 2014; Schumpeter, 2015), Babina (2020) exposes a micro-foundational mechanism of “destructive creation”. Here, high-human-capital individuals leverage unique networks built with corporate clients during prior employment to mobilise capital and launch distressed nascent ventures specifically during economic downturns, managing risk-taking behaviour (Rampini, 2004) when traditional, high-salaried paths fracture (Andrade and Kaplan, 1998).

Consequently, for a nascent student innovator operating in a stable economy, transitioning from passive intent to active venture creation requires specific cognitive distortions to override the gravity of a high reservation wage. Following the behavioural bias taxonomy of Nobre et al. (2022), the final choice to execute an entrepreneurial action relies on three primary behavioural distortions that override rational economic calculations with cross-referenced details outlined in Table 15:
	Optimism and Overconfidence: Potential creators systematically underestimate market risks and overestimate their personal capabilities (Nobre et al., 2022).  However, this distortion is heavily influenced by demographic and sociological realities; structural perceptions of opportunity vary wildly across demographic lines (Davis and Shaver, 2012) and pervasive masculine stereotypes around entrepreneurial traits systematically suppress female career choices by artificially deflating their perceived behavioural control (Gupta et al., 2009).
	Loss Aversion: While loss aversion traditionally keeps risk-averse individuals in secure corporate roles, it can invert into a profound fear-of-missing-out (“FoMO”) (Gupta and Sharma, 2021) regarding a disruptive, time-sensitive market window within high-velocity tech ecosystems. Hayton and Cholakova (2012) explain how a student’s intention to pursue an innovative idea alters their cognitive judgment and decision-making by using explicit valence beliefs to reduce ambient market uncertainty.
	Self-attribution Bias: Nascent innovators frequently attribute localised market successes to their unique competencies while dismissing potential failures as external institutional friction (Nobre et al., 2022). Within the upper echelons, this self-attribution can manifest as extreme personality configurations; Engelen et al. (2016) conclude that executive narcissism (Emmons, 1987) drastically drives aggressive EO, sacrificing strategic flexibility and leading to extreme, high-volatility firm performance outcomes.

Ultimately, rather than operating as isolated psychological anomalies, these behavioural distortions are fuelled by affective engines. As articulated by Cardon et al. (2013), multi-dimensional EP—specifically passion for inventing, founding, and developing—serves as the emotional catalyst that sustains this vital optimism bias, providing the psychological insulation necessary to reject a stable corporate salary.

However, the structural nature of this identity matters immensely, introducing critical boundary conditions. Incorporating identity frameworks (Stets and Burke, 2000; Stryker and Burke, 2000), Huyghe et al. (2016) survey doctoral and post-doctoral scholars (n=2,308) to uncover a vital paradox; while obsessive pedagogical passion positively predicts internal academic spin-off behaviours, it acts as a severe inhibitor to open, nascent entrepreneurship outside the university walls.

Compounding this emotional complexity, Kibler et al. (2024) draw on the theory of social and emotional aging (Charles and Carstensen, 2009) to operationalise the “Hebe effect”, proving through longitudinal data (n=840) that an individual’s subjective perception of being biologically younger serves as a critical emotional buffer against burnout, sustaining the psychological well-being required to navigate long-term venture uncertainty.

Contextual Paradox: HK’s Institutional and Ecosystem Landscape
Regional Milieu versus the Stanford Incubation Benchmark
The operationalization of both passion and cognitive biases does not occur in a vacuum; it is strictly bounded by the surrounding entrepreneurial ecosystem. To properly evaluate HK’s landscape, it must be benchmarked against a mature, high-density Western archetype: the Stanford University incubation system.

The Stanford ecosystem represents an environment where the surrounding institutional architecture minimises the economic and social penalties of venture failure (Etzkowitz, 2022). Stanford graduates face an unexceptionally high reservation wage due to immediate access to lucrative Silicon Valley corporate careers (Mueller and Hall, 2012). Yet, the ecosystem successfully counters this pull through an unmatched concentration of venture capital, specialised intellectual property legal structures via active technology transfer offices (“TTOs”), dense corporate–academic knowledge spillovers, and low social stigma regarding failed ventures.

The mechanics of this high-end Western archetype are heavily documented in global datasets. Hsu (2007) utilises data from the MIT and Harvard Entrepreneurship Laboratories (n=149) to show that human and social capitals act as powerful complements (Gompers et al., 2006), driving a “celebrity effect” that exponentially inflates startup valuations and secures elite venture capital (“VC”) sourcing. Similarly, O’Shea et al. (2005) apply the resource-based view (“RBV”) (Wernerfelt, 1984) to the Association of University Technology Managers (“AUTM”) academic spin-off data (n=987), confirming that institutional support, historical university knowledge accumulation, and learning effects are the true drivers of successful commercialization technology transfers.

In sharp contrast to this supportive Western template, HK’s milieu presents a profound contextual paradox. While local university students are surrounded by a wealthy economy, the local labour market offers highly lucrative, low-risk alternative pathways (e.g., global banking, real estate conglomerates, and stable civil service roles) that drive reservation wages upward (Bickenbach et al., 2017; McMullen et al., 2008). Unlike the Stanford model, HK’s institutional architecture possesses deep cultural–cognitive, structural, and relational inhibitors (Gümüsay and Bohné, 2018; Man et al., 2002).

Historically rooted in its development from a British colony to a Special Administrative Region, HK’s commercial legacy has favoured a conservative, Kirznerian approach to business—focusing on rapid market arbitrage, short-term financial flexibility, property speculation, and manufacturing offshoring (Lo et al., 2023; Yu, 2000). Radical, Schumpeterian technological creation remains constrained by immense real estate friction, family face-saving pressures, and a deep social stigma attached to business failure. 

The empirical manifestations of these constraints are distinct within the local subsample. Uctu and Jafta (2012) utilise qualitative case designs across specialised HK biochemical academic spin-offs (n=5) to show that acute deficits in specialised, long-term financial resources constitute an almost insurmountable obstacle to sustainable regional academic entrepreneurship. Furthermore, where student-managed accelerators are introduced to bridge this gap, they face deep structural trade-offs; Adomdza (2016) demonstrates that while student-managed accelerator models offer superior operational flexibility for raw innovation, they frequently compromise long-term commercial profitability and venture sustainability. 

This institutional friction is further illuminated by Eesley et al. (2016) through a large-scale evaluation of elite Tsinghua University alumni (n=2,966) under China’s “Project 985” tech-transfer initiative. They find that top-down institutional mandates often trigger unexpected policy inconsistencies and stall performance, arguing that only a radical revolutionizing of innovation and EE can insulate nascent founders from erratic regulatory structures.

Cross-boundary Knowledge Spillovers and Institutional Catalysts
To break this institutional inertia, regional policymakers have introduced macro-environmental catalysts, specifically HK’s strategic integration into GBA innovation corridor. The GBA framework aims to replicate Western agglomeration effects (Glaeser, 2007), combining HK’s elite academic research capacity with the deep manufacturing, capitalisation, and scaling supply chains of PRC.

This cross-boundary integration alters the mechanics of knowledge transfer (“KT”) and strategic venture orientation. As Mok and Jiang (2020) argue, the structural optimisation of HK’s entrepreneurship ecosystem relies on building a complex multiple-networking framework across government, business, and academia. This cross-industry integration facilitates a process of “ecosystem hybridisation”, allowing knowledge flows to bypass localised constraints.

To map this hybridization, Valdez and Richardson (2013) apply Scott’s (1995) institutional framework to GEM country-level datasets, proving that normative and cultural–cognitive institutional pillars exert a far more powerful influence on opportunity-motivated entrepreneurship (“OME”) versus necessity-motivated entrepreneurship (“NME”) than raw regulatory or economic factors. This underscores the value of regional social environments; Muscio and Vallanti (2024) utilise a massive archival panel of postgraduates (n=9,062) to prove that social proximity and gender-balanced academic environments drastically mitigate participation gaps, driving inclusive innovation through peer-group modelling.

Furthermore, when moving into high-tech innovation corridors, traditional linear performance models no longer apply. Empirical evidence from Tang et al. (2008) reveals that the relationship between EO and firm performance in emerging China is distinctly curvilinear, contrasting sharply with the stable, linear paths documented in mature Western markets. To navigate this non-linear volatility, Wang (2008) demonstrates through a mixed-methods design (n=213) that learning orientation (“LO”) functions as a vital internal mediator that stabilises and secures the EO–performance nexus, ensuring that aggressive risk-taking transitions into long-term financial returns. This requirement for multidimensional organizational capital is corroborated by Wu et al. (2008), who combine the RBV with social capital theory (“SCT”) within a Chinese cultural context (n=186) to prove that sustainable technological competitiveness depends on the dual networking capabilities of institutional trust and collaborative technical resource sharing.

This complex structural dynamic requires tracing the micro-foundations of stakeholder support. Edelman et al. (2016) draw on the micro-foundations of entrepreneurial action (Shepherd, 2015) using a massive Global University Entrepreneurial Spirit Students’ Survey (“GUESSS”) dataset (n=12,399) to confirm that instrumental financial, social, and emotional support from immediate networks is the primary mechanism facilitating a young adult’s active transition from intent into nascent founder status. In institutional settings, this support must be explicitly organised through entrepreneurial support organizations (“ESOs”). Bergman and McMullen (2022) conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis (n=337) categorising ESOs into five distinct archetypes—startup incubators, university science parks, venture accelerators, maker spaces, and co-working offices—proving that maximizing development relies on a delicate, strategic balance between the ESO’s institutional integration and the individual entrepreneur’s operational autonomy (Ellerman, 2005; McGregor, 1960).

This integration is critical when transforming university research into commercial products (Knockaert et al., 2011; Zellmer-Bruhn et al., 2021), where clear alignments must be built between TTOs, transitioning scientists, and incoming VC investors to avoid damaging faultlines across the stakeholder teams (Lau and Murnighan, 2005; Lim et al., 2013). To contextualise these support mechanisms within the regional landscape, (Ng et al., 2021) survey HK’s tertiary institutions (n=236) to show that integrating work-integrated learning (“WIL”) directly into the academic entrepreneurship framework significantly anchors student EI while building broader corporate employability skillsets.

Ultimately, as established by Patzelt and Shepherd (2011) using entrepreneurial action concepts (McMullen and Shepherd, 2006), the recognition and execution of sustainable development opportunities require the deep integration of natural and communal knowledge with technical expertise, structurally supported by individual threat perception, self-determination (Ryan and Deci, 2000), and reciprocal altruism (Batson et al., 1987). Where these institutional and cognitive alignments are achieved, social and environmental movements can systematically drive structural market conversions (Sine and Lee, 2009). Under extreme conditions, these combinations allow marginalised cohorts to leverage non-traditional career backgrounds to bypass labour market barriers, a phenomenon verified by (Zhao et al., 2025) through nationwide China Labor-forces Dynamics Survey (“CLDS”) longitudinal data (n=17,866) showing how specific historical capital accumulations alter risk attitudes to drive large-scale, sustainable entrepreneurship.

By evaluating PES through both macro-level cross-boundary integration (GIS) and micro-level campus support (TTO), this dissertation uncovers the precise mechanisms required to tip the behavioural scales, allowing passionate HK student creators to bridge the “intent–action gap” and execute sustainable venture creation.

 Methodology
Chapter Overview
This chapter delineates the explanatory sequential mixed-methods architecture engineered to empirically evaluate the PEM within the unique socio-cognitive landscape of students in HK and the broader GBA. Grounded in a post-positivist paradigm, this study deploys an integrated, two-phase chronological design wherein qualitative insights directly decode the statistical boundaries of a quantitative path model. The chapter begins by mapping the core theoretical frameworks before outlining the quantitative core (n≥135) and a purposive qualitative sampling strategy brokered via Development and Alumni Affairs Office (“DAAO”) of the Hong Kong Metropolitan University (“HKMU”). Finally, the discussion specifies the 37-item context-adapted psychometric instruments, a six-block operational timeline, and rigorous governance protocols for data management, systematic data analysis, research ethics, and results presentation.

Theoretical Framework
The structural architecture is grounded in a post-positivist, socio-cognitive paradigm that blends “cold” cognitive frameworks with “hot” affective drivers to explain behaviour within a specific institutional environment. To mathematically evaluate and qualitatively contextualise the mechanisms translating student career intentions into actual IVC, the PEM synthesises three foundational pillars, as exhibited in Table 14.

Research Design
This dissertation implements an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design to investigate the socio-cognitive dynamics of potential student entrepreneurs. Rather than treating quantitative and qualitative data as isolated streams, this design deploys two distinct, integrated chronological phases where the qualitative findings directly explain the statistical boundaries of the quantitative path model (see Figure 2).

Quantitative Core (Empirical Baseline)
The initial phase takes a deductive approach using macro-survey data to validate the structural paths of PEM. Relationships among individual traits, cognitive filters, affective passion, and environmental moderators are computed using PLS-SEM in SmartPLS (Ringle et al., 2024). This causal-predictive modelling utilises a two-step estimation and specification process (Anderson and Gerbing, 1988) to isolate significant path coefficients and interaction effects across student cohorts.

Qualitative Contextualisation (Nuance Engine)
Rooted in constructivist approach to grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006), the second phase uses an inductive method to address the “intent–action gap”. While quantitative data show what structural paths are statistically significant, they cannot capture the underlying anxieties or personal risk calculations of student creators facing capital investment hurdles. Using thematic analysis via NVivo (Braun and Clarke, 2006), semi-structured interviews explore the lived experiences, risk-taking behaviours, and socio-cultural pressures unique to HK’s landscape, providing an explanatory lens for the validated PLS-SEM coefficients.

Participants and Sampling
Quantitative Sampling Architecture
To establish sufficient statistical power for the PLS-SEM path analysis, the minimum sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009). The full measurement instrument comprises 37 items operationalising 10 distinct intent construct dimensions. For the structural path configuration, the maximum number of directional predictors pointing toward a single endogenous construct is five (5). Resolving the “Intent–action Gap” via Behavioural Economics
This study advances contemporary socio-cognitive literature by shifting the scholarly conversation from “cold” cognitive intention models to an integrated, affective–behavioural framework. By infusing foundational insights from behavioural economics and cognitive bias theory, this research models the venture entry choice not as a homogeneous rational calculation, but as an intense psychological conflict between localised labour economics and individual cognitive distortions. By measuring PES across universities through both macro-level GBA infrastructure integration and micro-level institutional TTOs, this study uncovers how localised real estate friction, family face-saving dynamics, and academic resource allocation either support or suppress a student’s willingness to execute Schumpeterian creation behaviours.
